Municipal ferry returns to the river after nineteen years

The first municipal ferry to cross this stretch of the river since 2007 left the eastern ghat at 6:40 on Saturday morning carrying eleven passengers, two bicycles and a considerable quantity of local scepticism. It arrived on the far bank nine minutes later, which is roughly a quarter of the time the road bridge takes at that hour.

The service was cancelled nineteen years ago after the last operator withdrew, citing maintenance costs that the municipal board of the day declined to underwrite. What followed was not a debate but a slow forgetting. A generation grew up treating the two banks as separate towns, and the shops along the eastern ghat adjusted their stock accordingly.

Restoring the crossing took four years and three false starts. The first plan collapsed over the landing stage, which sits on land whose ownership has been disputed since partition. The second stalled when the boat identified for purchase failed a survey. The third, and the one that worked, was smaller in every dimension than its predecessors: one vessel, two landings, a single crew.

Fares have been set at eight rupees, with a monthly pass at one hundred and forty for anyone who can show a local address. The board expects the route to run at a modest loss for its first two years and has budgeted accordingly, a candour that has drawn less criticism than officials privately anticipated.

Not everyone is satisfied. Traders on the western bank have asked for a second morning sailing before seven, arguing that the current timetable delivers customers after their busiest hour has passed. The transport committee has agreed to review the schedule in November once three months of ridership figures are available.

For now the boat runs six times a day, and on Saturday afternoon it was full on every crossing. Whether that holds through the monsoon, when the current here is a serious proposition rather than a scenic one, is the question the committee will actually be answering in November.

The eastern landing will be lit by the end of September. Until then the last sailing leaves at half past five, and the crew have been instructed not to wait for latecomers.
